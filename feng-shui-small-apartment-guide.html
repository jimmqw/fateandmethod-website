Feng Shui for a Small Apartment: What Actually Applies

Most feng shui advice assumes a house with a garden. Here is the part that survives a sixty-square-metre rental.

Most feng shui advice assumes a house: a front door, a garden, a commanding position. Most people live in a flat of sixty square metres with one window and a door that opens onto a corridor. This guide is about the second case.

What changes in a small apartment

Three classical concerns simply do not apply. You cannot choose the facing of a building you rent. You cannot place a water feature. You cannot build a screen wall. What remains is the part of the tradition that was always about the interior: where you sleep, where you sit, what you can see, and what is behind you.

The four things that actually matter

ConcernWhat it means in practiceTypical fix
Commanding positionYou can see the door from where you sleep or work without being in line with itAngle the desk; move the bed off the door axis
BackingA solid wall behind your seat, not a window or a walkwayTurn the desk; add a tall shelf as a virtual wall

1. The commanding position, for renters

The classical rule is that the occupant should see the entrance from the main seat without sitting directly in line with it. In a small flat this usually means one of two moves: angle the desk so the door is in peripheral vision, or place the bed so the door is visible but not head-on.

What the rule is actually encoding is nervous system comfort: you are not startled, and you are not exposed. That is why it survives translation into a studio apartment.

2. Backing: the cheapest improvement

A solid wall behind you is the single most reliable adjustment in a small flat, and the most commonly ignored. Desks are pushed against windows for the view; the result is a seat with nothing behind it and a glare in front.

If there is no wall, a tall bookshelf behind the chair does the same job. It should be at least head height when seated, and it should be full.

3. Mirrors: where the advice goes wrong

Mirrors are recommended for dark corridors and condemned opposite the bed. Both rules come from the same observation: a mirror doubles what it faces. Facing a dark corridor, it doubles the light. Facing a bed, it doubles the occupant — which is why the classical texts treat it as disruptive to sleep.

Practical version for a small flat: a mirror at the end of a corridor, none directly opposite the bed, and none that reflects a cluttered corner.

4. The entry: one metre of clarity

Almost every small flat fails here, because the door is where shoes, bags and deliveries land. The tradition treats the entrance as the mouth of the home; the practical instruction is that the first metre inside should be clear, lit and not blocked by furniture.

A closed shoe cabinet and a single warm lamp solve it. This is the cheapest change in the list and the one guests feel immediately.

5. What not to bother with in a rental

  • Bagua grid mapping. Overlaying nine sectors on a floor plan and assigning life areas is a twentieth-century simplification. It is harmless as a checklist and misleading as a system.
  • Lucky-object shopping. A crystal does nothing that a lamp and a clear surface do not do better.
  • Facing-direction calculations for the building. You did not choose the building and you are not going to move because of a compass reading.

If you want the underlying theory rather than the checklist, our feng shui basics guide covers the classical vocabulary, and the yin and yang guide explains why balance rather than accumulation is the goal. For the personal chart side — which is a different system — see the Ba Zi beginner's guide.

A one-afternoon checklist

  1. Stand at your desk. Can you see the door without turning your head more than forty-five degrees?
  2. Put your hand behind your chair. Is there a wall or a full shelf within reach?
  3. Open the front door. Is the first metre clear, lit and unblocked?
  4. Lie on the bed. Is there a mirror reflecting you? Move it if so.
  5. Look at the darkest corner you use daily. Can it get daylight or a lamp?

Why the classical texts assumed a courtyard house

Nearly every feng shui manual was written for a building type that most readers will never occupy: a rectangular courtyard house with a gate, a screen wall, a main hall and a rear garden. The rules assume you can orient the building, control the entrance, and place water.

When those assumptions fail, the advice does not become wrong so much as inapplicable. The useful move is to translate each rule into the concern it encodes, then apply the concern to the space you have.

"The gate should not face the main door directly" encodes privacy and the reduction of through-draught and through-traffic. In a flat, that becomes: the entry should not open straight onto the living space with no transition. A shoe cabinet and a lamp do that job.

"Water should be placed to gather qi" encodes the value of a focal point that slows movement. In a flat, that becomes: something at the end of a corridor that gives the eye a rest, rather than a blank wall or a mirror bouncing light.

Bedroom specifics for small flats

The bedroom carries most of the practical weight in a small apartment, because it is the room you cannot avoid using.

Bed position. Feet should not point directly at the door. If the room is too narrow to avoid it, angle the bed or place a low bench at the foot. The classical reason is exposure; the practical reason is that a bed in the door line catches every draught and every light from the corridor.

Under-bed storage. The tradition dislikes it, and the practical objection is real: sealed storage under a bed reduces airflow and traps dust. If you need the space, use closed boxes and air the room weekly.

Electronics. A phone charging beside the pillow is the modern equivalent of the mirror problem — reflected light and attention at the hour when both should be absent. Move the charger across the room.

Work-from-home corners

Since 2020 the most common feng shui question is about a desk in a bedroom. The classical answer is not to do it; the practical answer is to create a boundary.

Options in order of effectiveness: a separate room, a screen, a tall shelf, a rug that defines the zone, and finally a lamp that is only on during work hours. The point is that the same surface should not be both desk and dinner table without a transition, because the mind does not switch contexts on command.

Backing matters more here than anywhere else. A desk facing a wall with the door behind you is the worst arrangement in the list; a desk with a wall behind and the door in peripheral vision is the best available in most flats.

How to evaluate advice you read online

Feng shui content on the internet is dominated by listicles that tell you to buy something. Three questions filter most of it out.

Does the recommendation describe a mechanism? "Place a plant in the southeast for wealth" gives no mechanism. "A plant softens a hard corner and adds humidity" does. The first is decoration with a promise attached; the second is an observation you can verify.

Does it assume you can change the building? If the advice requires a renovation, it is not for a renter. Most small-apartment readers are renters.

Does it cost money to follow? The most reliable adjustments — moving a desk, clearing the entry, adding a lamp — are free or cheap. Advice that begins with a purchase should be treated with suspicion until the free version has been tried.

A note on the two systems people confuse

Feng shui is about space. Ba Zi is about time — the moment you were born. They are different systems with different inputs, and mixing them produces contradictory instructions: the compass says your bedroom should face east, the chart says your favourable element is water, and neither has anything to say about the other.

Choose one frame for a given question. Use feng shui for "where should the desk go" and Ba Zi for "when should I start this project". Applied to the same decision they will disagree, and the disagreement is not informative.

Finally, a test of whether any of this helped: spend a week in the changed arrangement and notice whether you sit down more easily at the desk, or sleep more quickly. If the answer is no, move things again. The tradition's own instruction is to observe the result, not to trust the rule.

FAQ

Can feng shui be applied to a rented flat?

Yes, but only the interior part. Placement, light, backing and clutter are all within a tenant's control; building orientation and exterior features are not.

Is the bagua map reliable?

It is a modern simplification and varies between schools. Use it as a prompt to look at each room, not as a measurement.

Does a mirror really need to avoid the bed?

The classical texts say so, and the practical reason — reflected movement and light disrupting sleep — is easy to verify for yourself. It costs nothing to move it.

What if my flat is a single room?

Then separate functions rather than rooms: a screen or shelf between sleeping and working zones, and one lamp per zone. The tradition's concern is that rest and activity do not share the same space without a boundary.
